> **TL;DR.** The standard post-training playbook (run SFT, pick the best checkpoint by pass@1, then apply GRPO) reliably selects the *worst* RL starting point once the model has been overtrained.
>
> - **Why it's structural:** pass@1 rewards low-entropy, confident distributions, while GRPO needs diversity across rollouts to produce any gradient signal.
> - **The divergence:** on a Qwen2.5-Coder-3B depth ladder, pre-RL pass@1 rises with SFT depth (0.151 → 0.187) while peak GRPO pass@10 falls monotonically (0.806 → 0.481).
> - **The fix:** a single forward pass measuring pre-RL token entropy identifies the failure before any RL compute is spent.

Throughout, one checkpoint plays the antagonist: the **5.8-epoch Qwen checkpoint**, the one the standard rule picks as *best*, and the one GRPO can do the least with.

**Notation.** Everything downstream is built from these quantities.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $H$ | mean next-token entropy at $T=1.0$ (nats), how spread out the sampling distribution is |

## The selection problem

You just ran supervised fine-tuning, and the folk wisdom is simple: pick the checkpoint with the best pass@1 and hand it to RL. This story is not so much wrong as *backwards*. Once SFT has run long enough, the highest-pass@1 checkpoint is the one GRPO can do the **least** with, and you can lose an entire RL run, hundreds of GPU-hours, on a checkpoint that was doomed before the first gradient step.

The dominant post-training pipeline for code and reasoning models is: run supervised fine-tuning (SFT), apply reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) via GRPO [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300), and initialize RL from the SFT checkpoint with the highest pass@1. The logic is intuitive: better pre-RL capability should translate to a better RL starting point. The experiments in this post show that the logic is wrong, and wrong in a predictable, measurable way. Concretely, we establish three things:

- **an exact threshold.** Under binary rewards the within-group advantage variance is $\mathbb{E}[\sigma_G^2] = p(1-p)(g-1)/g$, which pins down a pass@1 value $p^*(g)$ below which GRPO's group-relative signal structurally collapses.
- **a rank inversion.** On Qwen2.5-Coder-3B, deeper SFT *raises* pre-RL pass@1 while *lowering* peak GRPO pass@10: the highest-pass@1 checkpoint loses to shallower ones in every seed.
- **a safe regime.** On DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B, pass@1 stays far above $p^*(8)$ and RL ranks compress rather than invert, drawing the boundary between the two outcomes.

**Why high pass@1 implies low entropy.** Checkpoint selection scores pass@1 with a single near-greedy decode, so the metric rewards concentrated mass (every $p$ reported in this post is measured at GRPO's rollout temperature $T = 1.0$ instead, which is the number the gradient actually depends on). A model that scores well under greedy decoding must concentrate its probability mass on the correct token at nearly every position: the more confident the distribution, the lower the mean next token entropy $H$. SFT with cross-entropy loss directly optimizes for this; it increases the log-probability of correct continuations, compressing the output distribution. Extended SFT does not just make the model more accurate; it makes the model more *certain*, driving $H$ below what greedy decoding strictly requires.

**Why GRPO needs the opposite.** GRPO [[1]] estimates policy advantage from the *spread of rewards within a group* of $g$ rollouts per prompt, with no learned value baseline. Let $p$ denote per prompt pass@1 under GRPO's rollout temperature ($T = 1.0$). If the model samples $g$ completions and they are all identical, their rewards are identical, the within group advantage is zero for every token, and the policy gradient vanishes. The within group advantage variance $\sigma_G^2$ is the quantity GRPO actually learns from, and it requires distributional diversity at sampling time.

**The structural tension.** These two requirements conflict directly when SFT has compressed the output distribution below a critical entropy threshold. A checkpoint with high pass@1 has low $H$; low $H$ at $T = 1.0$ means homogeneous rollouts; homogeneous rollouts mean $\sigma_G^2 \to 0$; and $\sigma_G^2 = 0$ means no gradient. The checkpoint the standard rule identifies as *best* is the one GRPO has the least traction on.

Prior work shows that prolonged SFT tends to memorize rather than generalize [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17161), that pass@1 is a weak predictor of post-RL performance [[3]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01939), and that reward-driven entropy collapse destabilizes multi-turn agent RL [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12599). We show something sharper: on a controlled depth ladder, the checkpoint with the *highest* pass@1 achieves the *worst* RL outcome across all seeds. The inversion is predictable from a single entropy measurement before any RL compute is spent.

The temperature mismatch also makes pass@$k$ at large $k$ a poor selection signal. Pass@$k$ asks whether a problem is *ever* solvable in $k$ tries; it captures capability ceiling but not sampling reliability. Two checkpoints with the same pass@64 but different entropy profiles behave very differently under GRPO; the checkpoint specific risk is by construction outside pass@$k$'s scope.

### Experimental setup

We hold architecture, data, hyperparameters, and evaluation fixed and vary only SFT training duration.

- **Primary ladder:** Qwen2.5-Coder-3B-Base at five checkpoints (1.0, 1.9, 2.9, 3.8, and 5.8 epochs).
- **Contrastive ladder:** DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B-Base at four matched depths, giving a second regime to test against.
- **Fine-tuning:** LoRA on 5,000 decontaminated KodCode-V1 problems for both ladders.
- **RL:** 400 steps of GRPO (DAPO variant [[5]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476), group size $g = 8$, no KL penalty) under a binary unit test reward (+2.0 if all tests pass, else 0).
- **Metrics:** pass@$\{1, 10\}$ via the unbiased estimator from Chen et al. [[6]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374), and next token entropy $H$ from a single forward pass on prompt tokens at $T = 1.0$.

On the Qwen ladder, the two metrics diverge as SFT deepens:

- **Pre-RL pass@1 rises** from 0.151 to 0.187; our antagonist, the 5.8-epoch checkpoint, looks *best* by the standard selection criterion.
- **Peak GRPO pass@10 falls** from 0.806 to 0.481 (3 seed mean, $n = 20$); that same 5.8-epoch checkpoint is last in every individual seed, not just in expectation.

The inversion is not a fragile averaging artifact. It holds from the very first evaluation at step 50, across all three seeds, at every intermediate SFT depth. The 1.0 epoch policy peaks at pass@10 = 0.824 at step 250 and stays within 5% through step 400. The 5.8 epoch policy peaks at 0.527 at step 50 and never recovers (both seed 42; the 3-seed means in Table 1 are 0.806 and 0.481). Its pass@1 declines from 0.085 at step 50 to 0.020 by step 200, a level at which the GRPO learning signal has effectively vanished.

Two secondary observations sharpen the mechanism. First, the **peak step contracts** monotonically with SFT depth: from step 250 (1.0 epochs) to step 50 (5.8 epochs). Deeper checkpoints do not learn faster; they exhaust their gradient signal sooner. Second, across the full ladder ($n = 15$, five checkpoints × three seeds), pre-RL pass@1 predicts peak pass@10 at Spearman $\rho = -0.75$, while pre-RL entropy $H$ predicts it at $\rho = +0.69$ (both $p < 0.01$, seed demeaned). The standard signal points the wrong direction; entropy points the right one.

**Takeaway:** the standard rule doesn't merely fail to help; in the overtrained regime it actively selects the worst checkpoint on the ladder. The next section explains why this happens with such regularity, by unpacking what GRPO's gradient actually depends on.

## Mechanism: the entropy collapse spiral

GRPO learns only from **disagreement**. It can push the policy toward answers that *some* rollouts in a group got right and *others* got wrong; if every rollout in a group agrees, there is nothing to compare and nothing to learn. When SFT compresses the output distribution, the $g$ sampled completions become nearly identical, their rewards become nearly identical, and the group-relative advantage (along with its gradient) vanishes. The rest of this section makes "nearly identical" quantitative.

<details className="derivation">
<summary>Derivation: from Bernoulli rewards to the learning signal</summary>

The variance of a set of 0/1 values with mean $\bar{r}$ is exactly $\bar{r}(1 - \bar{r})$. For a group of $g$ rollouts under a binary reward with per prompt success probability $p$, rewards are $r_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ and the GRPO advantage is $A_i = r_i - \bar{r}$. Tracking the group variance $\textcolor{#8c1515}{\sigma_G^2}$ (in red) through the expectation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textcolor{#8c1515}{\sigma_G^2}
  &= \tfrac{1}{g}\textstyle\sum_i (r_i - \bar{r})^2
  && \text{group advantage variance} \\
  &= \bar{r}\,(1 - \bar{r})
  && \text{fact 1: binary rewards } r_i \in \{0,1\} \\
\mathbb{E}\big[\textcolor{#8c1515}{\sigma_G^2}\big]
  &= p\,(1 - p)\,\frac{g-1}{g}
  && \text{expectation over } S=\textstyle\sum_i r_i \sim \text{Binomial}(g, p)
\end{aligned}
$$

</details>

<div className="callout callout-key">
<span className="callout-label">Key result</span>

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\sigma_G^2\big] = p\,(1 - p)\,\frac{g-1}{g}
$$

The learning signal is a downward parabola in $p$, pinned to zero at $p \in \{0, 1\}$. A group teaches GRPO nothing unless its rollouts *disagree* on reward, and disagreement is exactly what a low-entropy, high-pass@1 checkpoint has trained away.

</div>

In words: the gradient does not fade gently as the policy sharpens; it is *structurally* absent whenever every rollout in a group shares the same reward. That happens with probability $f_g(p) = p^g + (1-p)^g$. In a group of 8, if all 8 succeed or all 8 fail, the advantage is zero for every token; there is nothing for GRPO to reinforce.

<details className="derivation">
<summary>Full derivation: the majority-degenerate threshold $p^*(g)$</summary>

Define the **majority degenerate threshold** $p^*(g)$ as the pass@1 below which more than half of all groups are degenerate: $f_g(p^*) = 0.5$. Setting $f_g(p^*) = 0.5$ and expanding $(1-p)^g \approx e^{-gp}$, which holds for small $p$:

$$
p^{*g} + e^{-gp^*} \approx 0.5 \implies e^{-gp^*} \approx 0.5 \implies p^*(g) \approx \frac{\ln 2}{g}
$$

The $p^{*g}$ term is $O(2^{-g})$ and drops out for the group sizes used in practice.

</details>

<div className="callout callout-key">
<span className="callout-label">Key result</span>

$$
p^*(g) \approx \frac{\ln 2}{g}
$$

Below roughly $(\ln 2)/g$ pass@1, more than half your rollout groups are degenerate; they teach GRPO nothing. Because $p^*$ shrinks only as $\Theta(1/g)$, doubling the rollout budget merely halves the threshold: a policy already deep in collapse cannot buy back trainability with more samples.

</div>

For $g = 8$: $p^*(8) = 0.083$. The 5.8 epoch Qwen policy crosses this threshold by step 200, where its pass@1 has fallen to $p = 0.020$. The threshold is a lookup, not a curiosity; here it is for the group sizes people actually use:

| $g$ | $p^*(g)$ | $\mathbb{E}[\sigma_G^2]$ at $p^*$ | Practical reading |
|:---|---:|---:|:---|
| 4 | 0.159 | 0.100 | need >15.9% pass@1 |
| 8 | 0.083 | 0.067 | need >8.3% pass@1 |
| 16 | 0.042 | 0.038 | need >4.2% pass@1 |
| 32 | 0.021 | 0.020 | need >2.1% pass@1 |

<p className="table-caption">Table 2: The majority-degenerate threshold for standard GRPO group sizes. Look up your $g$; if per-prompt pass@1 at $T=1.0$ sits below $p^*(g)$, most groups carry no gradient. Source: Aphale &amp; Liu, 2026, Appendix A.3.</p>

**The reinforcing cycle.** The diagram below composes these elements into a feedback loop; once entered, each stage amplifies the previous.

<figure>
  <img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/projects/passk-rl-readiness/static/images/fig_collapse_cycle.svg" alt="Five-stage entropy collapse spiral diagram" />
  <figcaption>The entropy collapse spiral. SFT overtraining sets the starting point below the safe operating band; each stage below amplifies the previous. Source: Aphale &amp; Liu, 2026.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Low SFT entropy.** Extended SFT compresses the output distribution: pre-RL entropy $H$ falls from 0.227 nats (1.0 epochs) to 0.120 nats (5.8 epochs). To calibrate: a uniform distribution over a 50,000 token vocabulary has entropy ~10.8 nats; a value of 0.120 nats means the model places nearly all its probability mass on a single token at almost every position, functionally reducing every sampling step to a greedy decode.
2. **Homogeneous rollouts.** At $T = 1.0$, the low entropy policy emits nearly identical completions, driving per prompt pass@1 $p$ toward zero.
3. **Reward variance collapse.** $\mathbb{E}[\sigma_G^2] \to 0$; at $p = 0.020$ it equals 0.017, with most groups degenerate.
4. **Gradient death.** With near-zero advantage variance the GRPO update is negligible; the policy cannot move toward correct solutions.
5. **Further compression.** Without gradient signal, optimizer momentum and weight decay erode capability, lowering $H$ still further and closing the loop.

The training curves show this spiral in practice. GRPO amplifies rather than equalizes the initial entropy gap: the worst token entropy ratio between the 1.0 and 5.8 epoch checkpoints grows from 4.3× at step 10 to 12.3× by step 400.

<div className="callout callout-intuition">
<span className="callout-label">Intuition</span>

This is a collapse in *sampling probability*, not in *capability*. Pass@64 stays in a narrow 0.897–0.932 band across the full ladder, and the diversity ratio $\Delta_k$ remains above 0.84. The deep checkpoints can still solve the problems; they have simply stopped *sampling* solutions often enough for GRPO to reinforce them. The knowledge is intact; the reach into it has narrowed.

</div>

**Takeaway:** entropy is both the cause of the collapse *and* measurable before RL starts. That makes it a free screening tool, the subject of the next section.

## A free pre-RL diagnostic

Entropy is measurable *before* any RL, so the failure mode can be screened at zero GRPO cost. We propose a two-stage protocol, and Table 3 shows it applied to the full Qwen ladder.

**Stage 1: Pre-RL entropy triage (no RL compute).** Measure mean next token entropy $H(\pi_\text{SFT})$ at rollout temperature $T = 1.0$ on a small probe set. Flag any checkpoint with $H < \tau_H$, where $\tau_H = 0.18$ nats on the Qwen ladder. Cost: a single forward pass on the probe set.

**Stage 2: Early GRPO entropy monitor (step 150).** After initiating RL, track the relative entropy drop $\Delta H(10 \to 150) / H(10)$ and abort if it exceeds 0.5. By step 150, the 5.8 epoch checkpoint has lost 64% of its entropy versus 24% for the 1.0 epoch checkpoint, a 2.7× difference sufficient to terminate the run before most compute is spent.

| SFT epochs | Stage 1: $H$ (nats) | Stage 2: drop<br/>by step 150 | Peak pass@10 |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.0 | 0.249 | 24% | 0.806 |
| 1.9 | 0.163 | 44% | 0.750 |
| 2.9 | 0.137 | 56% | 0.671 |
| 3.8 | 0.192 | 28% | 0.608 |
| 5.8 | 0.120 | 64% | 0.481 |

<p className="table-caption">Table 3: The two-stage diagnostic on Qwen2.5-Coder-3B (seed=42 calibration). Stage 1 flags checkpoints below $\tau_H = 0.18$ nats; Stage 2 flags entropy drops above 50%. Neither stage catches the 3.8 epoch rung ($H = 0.192$, 28% drop), the ladder's second-worst RL outcome, and Stage 1 also flags 1.9 epochs, its best post-RL rung: the screen is a risk signal, not a ranking. Source: Aphale &amp; Liu, 2026, Table 3.</p>

The threshold separates the ladder's extremes cleanly, with one exception in the middle.

<figure>
  <img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/projects/passk-rl-readiness/static/images/fig1_entropy_ladder.svg" alt="Bar chart of pre-RL next token entropy across the SFT depth ladder with threshold line" />
  <figcaption>Pre-RL next token entropy across the SFT depth ladder (HumanEval+ probe, T = 1.0). The dashed line marks τ_H = 0.18 nats. The ladder's extremes fall on the expected sides of it; the 3.8 epoch rung passes the screen and still loses ground under GRPO. Source: Aphale &amp; Liu, 2026, Fig. 1.</figcaption>
</figure>

The payoff is concrete. Stage 1 rejects the standard rule's pick outright: the 5.8 epoch checkpoint sits at $H = 0.120$ against $\tau_H = 0.18$. Screening on entropy instead leaves the 1.0 and 3.8 epoch rungs as candidates, and the highest-entropy survivor (1.0 epochs) reaches deep eval pass@10 of 0.597 against 0.553 for the pass@1 winner, and peak pass@10 of 0.806 against 0.481 ($n = 128$, 3 seed mean). Every Qwen rung still loses ground under GRPO (Table 5), so the screen changes which loss you take, and on peak pass@10 it changes the ranking outright. Stage 2 bounds the downside of runs already underway: stopping a flagged run at step 150 instead of step 400 recovers 62.5% of RL compute.

Why entropy and not pass@1 as the screening variable? Because on this ladder pre-RL pass@1 spans only 0.151 to 0.187 (too narrow to discriminate) while entropy spans 0.120 to 0.249 nats. The theoretically grounded quantity to screen on is $\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\sigma_G^2] = \hat{p}(1-\hat{p})(g-1)/g$; entropy is the practical proxy that actually separates the checkpoints. The correlations bear this out:

| Pre-RL predictor | Spearman $\rho$ vs. peak pass@10 |
|:---|---:|
| Pre-RL pass@1 | −0.75 (inverted) |
| $\mathbb{E}[\sigma_G^2]$ | −0.75 |
| Pre-RL entropy $H$ | +0.69 |
| Pre-RL pass@64 | +0.57 |
| Step-10 entropy $H(10)$ | +0.82 |

<p className="table-caption">Table 4: Which pre-RL signal predicts GRPO success ($n=15$, seed-demeaned; $|\rho|>0.52$ is $p<0.05$). Pass@1 is a strong *inverse* predictor; entropy predicts the right direction. Source: Aphale &amp; Liu, 2026, Appendix A.6.</p>

<div className="callout callout-warn">
<span className="callout-label">Watch out</span>

$\tau_H = 0.18$ nats is calibrated to the Qwen ladder; treat the *ordering and risk signal* as the transferable claim (lower pre-RL entropy predicts worse GRPO outcome), not the specific cutoff. The screen tells you which side of the boundary a checkpoint sits on; the number itself will move with model family, tokenizer, and domain.

</div>

**Takeaway:** one forward pass separates the ladder's extremes, and Stage 2 bounds the damage on runs already committed, though the 3.8 epoch rung shows the screen is a risk signal rather than a guarantee. The next section stress-tests the account on a model that never crosses the threshold.

## When it is safe: a contrastive regime

If the collapse mechanism is correct, DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B-Base under the identical recipe should show the opposite pattern: pre-RL pass@1 well above $p^*(8) = 0.083$, healthy $\sigma_G^2$ throughout training, and a positive rank correlation between SFT depth and RL outcome. Table 5 checks these predictions directly, rung by rung.

| Epochs | Pre pass@10 | Post pass@10 | Δ pass@10 | Headroom<br/>vs $p^*(8)$ |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| *Qwen2.5-Coder-3B* | | | | |
| 1.0 | 0.663 | 0.597 | −0.067 | 1.8× |
| 1.9 | 0.660 | 0.643 | −0.017 | 2.0× |
| 2.9 | 0.655 | 0.594 | −0.061 | 1.9× |
| 3.8 | 0.675 | 0.566 | −0.110 | 2.1× |
| 5.8 | 0.700 | 0.553 | −0.148 | 2.3× |
| *DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B* | | | | |
| 1.0 | 0.838 | 0.841 | +0.003 | 4.2× |
| 1.9 | 0.860 | 0.884 | +0.024 | 4.6× |
| 3.8 | 0.852 | 0.870 | +0.017 | 5.0× |
| 5.8 | 0.854 | 0.875 | +0.021 | 5.0× |

<p className="table-caption">Table 5: Cross-model deep-eval pass@10 ($n=128$, 3-seed mean). "Headroom" is pre-RL pass@1 divided by $p^*(8)=0.083$. Absolute pass@10 levels are not comparable across model families; within-ladder ordering is the comparison of interest. Every Qwen rung loses under GRPO ($\Delta<0$); every DeepSeek rung gains: the difference is which side of $p^*(8)$ they start on. Source: Aphale &amp; Liu, 2026, Table 2.</p>

Every prediction holds. DeepSeek's pre-RL pass@1 sits at least 4.2× above $p^*(8)$; within group reward variance stays at $\mathbb{E}[\sigma_G^2] \approx 0.20$ to 0.21 throughout, roughly 12× the Qwen 5.8 epoch value; and RL rank order is perfectly preserved ($\rho = +1.00$, vs. −0.75 on Qwen). The five-stage collapse cycle never initiates.

Both ladders independently identify 1.9 epochs as their SFT optimum, but only Qwen crosses the threshold. Rank inversion is a symptom of crossing the gradient-vanishing boundary, not of SFT depth per se. The result is not "deeper SFT is always harmful"; it is "deeper SFT is harmful when it pushes $p$ below $p^*(g)$," and the pre-RL diagnostic tells you which side you are on before any RL budget is committed.

**Takeaway:** the mechanism predicts *both* outcomes from one quantity: the threshold headroom. This raises an obvious follow-up: if the collapse is identifiable in advance, can a post hoc intervention fix it?

## Why the obvious fixes fail

Two natural interventions suggest themselves: penalize the policy for drifting (a KL term), or re-inject entropy directly (label smoothing). Table 6 and the figure below it show the results. Neither works, and understanding why reveals that the failure is upstream of anything the RL objective can address.

| SFT epochs | Intervention | Pre-RL pass@1 | Peak pass@10 |
|:---|:---|---:|---:|
| 1.0 | none ($\beta=0$) | 0.137 | 0.824 |
| 5.8 | none ($\beta=0$) | 0.187 | 0.527 |
| 1.0 | KL penalty ($\beta=0.01$) | 0.137 | 0.684 |
| 5.8 | KL penalty ($\beta=0.01$) | 0.187 | 0.461 |
| 5.8 | label smoothing ($\alpha=0.1$) | **0.600** | **0.214** |

**KL penalty.** Adding $\beta = 0.01$ to the GRPO objective regresses *both* checkpoints below their $\beta = 0$ baselines: peak pass@10 falls from 0.824 to 0.684 (1.0 epochs) and from 0.527 to 0.461 (5.8 epochs). Rank inversion is preserved. A full $\beta$ sweep is out of scope, but the structural account predicts that no KL coefficient restores within group reward variance: a KL term constrains the policy's deviation from a reference distribution but does not change the fact that rollouts at $T = 1.0$ are homogeneous when entropy is low. The reward variance problem is in the sampling distribution, not in the RL objective.

**Label smoothing** and the restoration paradox. Applying label smoothing to the 5.8 epoch checkpoint produces the highest pre-RL pass@1 of any run in the study (0.600) yet the lowest peak GRPO pass@10 (0.214). This is the clearest evidence that the failure is distributional.

<div className="callout callout-warn">
<span className="callout-label">Watch out</span>

High entropy is **necessary but not sufficient** for GRPO trainability. Label smoothing raises entropy by spreading probability mass *uniformly* across the vocabulary. Argmax ordering is preserved, so pass@1 looks excellent, but at $T = 1.0$ the non-argmax mass now lands on vocabulary *noise* rather than on plausible alternative continuations. The 8 rollouts sample mostly gibberish, almost all fail, $p \to 0$, and $\sigma_G^2 \to 0$ again, this time because the added mass went to the wrong place. The mass you add back must land on tokens that lead to correct solutions.

</div>

**Takeaway:** you cannot patch your way out of a collapsed checkpoint downstream. Both interventions operate on the RL objective or the marginal token distribution *after* the checkpoint has been set. The only reliable lever is choosing a checkpoint that keeps $p > p^*(g)$ to begin with.

---

## Takeaways

The core finding is a structural incompatibility in the standard SFT→RL pipeline. Pass@1 selection and GRPO trainability pull the output distribution in opposite directions, and the standard recipe has no mechanism to detect when they have diverged past the point of recovery.

What generalizes beyond this particular ladder:

- **The learning signal is reward *variance*, not reward.** GRPO can only learn from groups whose rollouts disagree; $\mathbb{E}[\sigma_G^2] = p(1-p)(g-1)/g$ is the whole story, and it is knowable before training.
- **There is an exact trainability floor.** $p^*(g) \approx (\ln 2)/g$ is a lookup, not a heuristic. Below it, most groups are degenerate, and more rollouts ($\Theta(1/g)$) barely help.
- **pass@1 is the wrong selection signal in the overtrained regime**, and it fails *predictably*: the RL outcome falls monotonically in SFT depth, in every seed, rather than noisily.
- **Capability and trainability are different axes.** A checkpoint can retain its pass@64 ceiling while losing the sampling reliability GRPO needs; entropy tracks the second axis, pass@$k$ tracks the first.

<div className="callout callout-recipe">
<span className="callout-label">The recipe</span>

1. **Screen (free):** measure mean next-token entropy $H$ at $T=1.0$ on a small probe. Flag any checkpoint with $H < \tau_H$ (calibrate $\tau_H$ per model; 0.18 nats on Qwen-3B).
2. **Select on entropy, not accuracy:** among checkpoints above threshold, stop SFT at maximum entropy compatible with your target pass@1, not at maximum pass@1. Keep $p > p^*(g) \approx (\ln 2)/g$.
3. **Monitor during RL:** track $\Delta H(10\to150)/H(10)$; abort if it exceeds 0.5 (recovers ~60% of the budget on a doomed run).
4. **Don't patch downstream:** KL penalties and label smoothing operate below $\sigma_G^2$ and will not rescue a collapsed checkpoint.

</div>

Open questions remain. The threshold $\tau_H = 0.18$ nats is calibrated on Qwen2.5-Coder-3B; whether it transfers across architectures and domains requires further study. It is also unclear whether RL algorithms with learned value baselines (PPO, REINFORCE with baseline) are more robust to entropy collapse; a critic that stabilizes advantage estimation might decouple pass@1 selection from RL readiness, and that comparison is a natural extension of this work.
